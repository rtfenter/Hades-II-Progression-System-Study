# Product Reasoning Log

This log captures how my understanding of Hades II's progression systems changed during the study and the product questions that emerged from it.

## Perspective

My observations come from a mix of playing Hades II and watching another player's progression.

I played on an already-progressed save after the main game had been completed, so I did not personally experience the intended fresh-save progression curve.

I use firsthand experience and observation here to generate questions rather than treating either as representative player research.

## Starting observation

What initially stood out to me was how much there was to discover despite the core run loop being relatively simple.

Early questions included:

- Which Boons work well together?
- Which weapons fit my playstyle?
- What can I unlock?
- What are the different resources for?
- What should I use at the Cauldron?
- Which Prophecies are worth pursuing?

Even after a failed run, I could understand more about what might work on the next attempt.

That made me interested in what actually persists when the run itself resets.

## Randomness vs. agency

My first instinct was that allowing players to choose their exact Boon build would make the game too deterministic.

The randomness forces experimentation and adaptation.

But complete randomness would create the opposite problem: what I learn about builds would be less useful if I couldn't act on that knowledge.

Keepsakes helped clarify the distinction.

The player gradually gains ways to steer a run without controlling its entire outcome.

### What this changed

I stopped thinking about randomness and player control as opposites.

The more useful principle became:

> **Agency doesn't require certainty.**

A player can gain meaningful control while uncertainty remains part of the experience.

## Why multiple resources?

I questioned why Hades II needs so many resources instead of one permanent currency.

A universal currency would be simpler.

It would also make it easier to find the most efficient earning path and direct everything toward a known optimal build.

Differentiated resources make progression interact with different systems and activities.

### What this changed

Resources aren't only prices.

Their sources can influence where players go and what systems they engage with, while their sinks determine what kinds of progression those activities feed.

That led me into thinking about the economy as a behavioral system rather than simply a set of costs.

## Incentivized behavior isn't automatically valuable behavior

That raised another question.

If players repeatedly complete an activity because an important resource is gated behind it, telemetry may show strong engagement.

But would they still choose that activity if the reward changed or disappeared?

That counterfactual matters.

### What this changed

I would not interpret repeated behavior alone as evidence that the activity is valuable to players.

I would want to understand why the behavior is happening before deciding the progression loop is successful.

## Pacing before tuning

I considered a hypothetical where players were obtaining an upgrade faster than intended.

The obvious intervention would be increasing its resource cost.

My questions were different:

- Is the upgrade meaningful?
- Why does its acquisition speed matter?
- What happens downstream when players receive it earlier?
- Does it affect difficulty or another progression system?
- Is cost actually the right intervention point?
- Could the issue be resource availability, upgrade power, or the intended pacing itself?

### What this changed

"Faster than intended" is an observation.

It isn't enough information to determine that the economy needs to become more restrictive.

The intended player experience has to explain why the pacing matters first.

## Arcana exposed three different constraints

Initially, I thought Grasp was the primary Arcana constraint.

Looking more closely showed three separate layers.

### Access

What has been revealed?

The board controls when cards enter the player's available decision space.

### Acquisition

What can I obtain or improve?

Resources determine which revealed options the player actually earns.

### Capacity

What can I activate together?

Grasp determines which owned options can be active simultaneously.

### What this changed

These aren't redundant restrictions.

They solve different progression problems.

Access controls the expansion of choice.

Acquisition creates prioritization and resource demand.

Capacity preserves tradeoffs after the player has earned the options.

## Progressive disclosure of choice

I initially connected Arcana's reveal structure to the same randomness-versus-determinism problem I had seen with Boons.

That wasn't quite right.

Arcana reveal order isn't primarily random.

It controls the sequence in which player choice expands.

A new player given every Arcana option immediately would technically have more choice, but might not yet understand the game well enough for those choices to be meaningful.

### What this changed

Progression isn't only:

> weak → strong

It can also be:

> **few decisions → understandable decisions → more sophisticated decisions**

Boons and Arcana therefore create different forms of agency.

Boons require adaptation under uncertainty.

Arcana requires prioritization within progressively expanding options.

## Progression vs. constraint

I initially assumed that enough Grasp might eventually allow players to activate everything they cared about.

That created an interesting tension.

Removing constraints can feel rewarding because the player has progressed beyond them.

But completely removing the capacity constraint would also eliminate much of the decision-making the Arcana system creates.

The actual system preserves loadout tradeoffs even at high Grasp.

### What this changed

The goal does not have to be preserving the same amount of friction forever.

A progression system can loosen a constraint while preserving its underlying purpose.

> **Progression expands freedom without necessarily eliminating meaningful tradeoffs.**

## Resource exhaustion

I also considered what happens when players accumulate resources they no longer have meaningful reasons to spend.

My first questions weren't about adding another expensive sink.

They were:

- Why did the surplus happen?
- Did we miscalculate source and sink rates?
- Did players stop valuing available sinks?
- Did the surplus change progression or runs in a meaningful way?
- Is the surplus actually causing a problem?

### What this changed

Resource surplus, like acquisition speed, is an observation before it is a problem.

The downstream effect determines whether intervention is necessary.

## Dominant strategies

Finally, I considered a hypothetical where most late-game players converge on essentially the same Arcana loadout.

I would consider that worth investigating, but I would not immediately nerf the dominant cards.

Possible explanations include:

- individual card balance
- weak alternatives
- Grasp costs
- weapon or Boon synergies
- encounter design
- Arcana having too much influence over run outcomes
- players copying an established meta despite viable alternatives

### What this changed

Convergence doesn't identify its own cause.

The product question becomes:

> **Why is this strategy dominant, what downstream behavior does that dominance create, and is that behavior harmful to the intended experience?**

## Final takeaway

I started by asking why repeated Hades II runs could remain meaningful despite resetting so much of the player's temporary power.

The system I ended up understanding more deeply was the relationship between progression and constraint.

Across Boons, resources, Arcana and Grasp, Hades II repeatedly increases the player's power, knowledge and agency without simply removing uncertainty or tradeoffs.

The most useful principle I took from the study was:

> **Progression becomes meaningful not because constraints disappear, but because the player gains increasingly informed ways to operate within them.**
